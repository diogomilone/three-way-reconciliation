Three-way reconciliation: matching the books, the payments and the bank each day: What went wrong

No rule flagged more than 1 of 20 invoices paid twice, because every record agreed, in a planted test.

The problems each rule missed, and why.

What every rule missed

  • An invoice paid twice, in two payments. Exact reference 0, amount-and-date fallback 1 and whole payment 0 of 20. The second payment has its own document, check and bank debit, so all three records agree. A duplicate-invoice check on supplier, invoice and amount before payment would catch it.
  • A late electronic payment. Cleared 8 business days after issue, it passed the 5-day window. Exact reference and whole payment flagged all 20, and the fallback 19, the wrong call for ordinary timing. The fallback's one pass came through a wrong pairing. One window for every payment type is too blunt.

What netting hid

  • A fee overcharge. Whole-batch matching flagged 0 of 20 batches charged a higher fee rate. The payout equalled the netted batch, so nothing disagreed. In the independent set, it also missed a processing fee charged twice, for the same reason.
  • Rounding of 1 to 3 cents. Exact reference and whole batch flagged all 20, the wrong call. The fallback rule's US$1.00 tolerance absorbed them, the right call, but the same tolerance would absorb a real 99-cent error.

What the fallback broke

All 543 wrong pairings came from the amount-and-date fallback, and it made no right one. It fires when a bank line finds no partner by reference. 499 times that was a check clearing after the 5-day window, whose true payment is older than the fallback looks. 44 times the payment was in reach but an earlier wrong pairing had taken it. It then takes the oldest open payment of nearly the same amount. In the planted test this hid 2 of 20 double-paid checks and 2 of 20 voided checks paid anyway. It also hid 2 of 20 checks the books never issued and 1 of 20 payments that never cleared.

The split of the 543 was counted in the second version, added after the results.

What went wrong in the study itself

  • One prediction partly missed: 46% of paper checks were flagged early at 5 days, not half.
  • The first version tested the fallback only behind a rule that refused check numbers more than 5 business days apart. So every pairing it made was wrong by design, and only the count came from the data. The second version tested it with check numbers hidden instead.
  • The expected finding said whole payment would make the most right calls on planted events. The fallback rule made more, 101 and 120.
  • In the second version, more wrong picks were expected at 15 days than at 5. There were slightly fewer.
  • The second version took the oldest of several look-alikes, so its rate measured that pick, not every amount-and-date rule. The third version measured matching only a lone fit.
  • In the third version, look-alikes were expected to be 25% to 45% of the debits in reach at 5 days. They were 18.3%. Paper debits were expected to draw more wrong picks than electronic ones; electronic drew more.
  • Two of the independent author's 24 events could not be applied as written. They put a number range on the check number, which the event grammar reads as text. They are reported as not applied.
  • 173 documents netting to zero would have counted as open money under every rule. They were left out before any scored run.

Limits

These are planted events on real records, written alongside the rules, 20 of each kind, and one independent set of 24.
