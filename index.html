Three-way reconciliation: matching the books, the payments and the bank each day

When checks missing their number were matched only if one payment fit, searching 12 weeks, not 1, cut wrong matches from 46 in 1,000 to 0.6.

Search 12 weeks back.

What to doWorthPutting it to workThree numbersWhat would change itLimitsDetail pages

What to do

Every day, finance matches the books, the payment run and the bank statement. Here three ways of matching them ran over a county's 13,101 bank debits, closing every business day. Then each debit's check number was hidden in turn.

  • Without a check number, match a debit only when one open payment fits, searching 12 weeks back. Electronic payments were never matched wrong: each cleared within 3 business days.
  • Never let the software choose between two that fit. Taking the oldest, 1 week back, was wrong for 47.5 in 1,000 paper checks whose payment was in the search. Searching further made it worse.
  • Pair a check with all the invoices it pays, and match their total to the bank line to the cent. Here 5,082 of 13,354 payments paid several invoices. Treat a card processor's payout the same way.
  • Wait about 3 weeks before chasing a paper check. With a 1-week wait, 46% were chased before they cleared.
  • Add two checks that matching cannot do: duplicate invoices before paying, since an invoice paid twice looks right everywhere, and processor fees against the contract rate.

What it is worth

Here, a 3-week wait instead of 1 week means 2,906 fewer needless chases of paper checks a year. Searching 12 weeks back instead of 1 means about 46 fewer wrong matches in every 1,000 paper checks that arrive without their number. It halves the debits sent to a person.

If each chase costs US$5 to US$25 of staff time, the fewer chases save US$14,530 to US$72,650 a year. The price: a check that never clears is caught 16 business days after issue, not 6.

Putting it to work

  • The reconciliation lead runs it beside the current matching for one month-end close, and names a cause for every difference.
  • Accounts payable owns the debits sent to a person, 23% of debits without a check number here, and samples the automatic matches monthly.
  • Treasury sets each payment type's wait from last year's clearing times.
  • Give the two outside checks an owner: duplicate invoices to accounts payable, processor fees to the contract owner.
  • At each close, report money left open as a share of money moved, breaks by kind, and matches made without a check number.

Three numbers that matter

  • 0.6 in 1,000. Paper checks without their number matched wrong, searching 12 weeks back, against 46.2 at 1 week.
  • 23%. Debits sent to a person, searching 12 weeks back, against 45% at 1 week.
  • 1,047 breaks. Payments still unmatched after a 3-week wait, against 3,953 at 1 week.

What would change the conclusion

The call rests on 0.6 wrong matches per 1,000 paper checks at 12 weeks. If a real bank statement showed 1 per 1,000 or more, I would send every debit without a check number to a person.

The test: match real paper-check debits that arrived without their number, and check each match against the check register. At 1 wrong per 1,000 or more, the call flips. To show the rate is under 1 four times in five takes 48,000 debits at the 0.6 seen here, and 184,000 at 0.8. A cheaper first check: rerun the county's other public years.

Limits

  • The bank lines are not a real statement. The payments and clearing dates are real, but each bank line is built from its payment, so the debits equal the paid total by construction. Unlike real ones, the lines carry no payment type. A single fit goes wrong here only when the true payment is older than the search. Real fees, partial amounts and type codes could move 0.6, and the share sent to a person, either way.
  • Check numbers were hidden one at a time. With many missing at once, wrong matches could rise or fall. The 12-week figure is 5 of 8,039 paper checks, with a 95% range of 0.2 to 1.5 per 1,000. It treats each check as independent, so it may be too narrow. So this data cannot yet show the rate is under 1.
  • One county, one year. The 12-week search follows this county's clearing times. Its other public years were not run.
  • Whole-payment matching's lead in money left open is built in. The one-to-one rules cannot pair a payment made of several invoice lines or batch rows.
  • The card processor is synthetic. No public source has all three records for one business.
  • The data gives each break's kind, not its cause: it records when a payment cleared, never why.
  • Nothing here ties to an outside figure. The status totals add to the year's US$605,400,363.83 by construction. The county's 2024 annual report puts General Fund spending at US$1,021,931,000; the General Fund payments here, US$294,472,660.79, are 28.8% of it. That spending also counts payroll, debt payments and costs owed but unpaid, and the report shows it by function, not by supplier payments. So public data cannot close the gap.
  • Tested as declared: the three rules, the daily close, the waits and the planted problems. Added after the first results, with predictions written first: the hidden-number test, its 12-week result and the break table. The hidden-number test replaced a flawed first comparison. The rule fixed before the run counted all debits: 0.4 per 1,000 at 12 weeks, the shortest under 1 either way.
  • Staff cost per chase is an estimate, set after the results, not measured. How often a bank line lacks its check number is not known, so the matching call is not priced.
  • Caught-or-missed figures come from planted problems, because real data lists none of its own errors. An independent author's 22 cases were too few to separate the rules.
